# Epic 3 Retrospective: Frame the Case and Build the Analytical Reading

Date: 2026-03-30
Epic Status: done
Retrospective Status: done

## Epic Review

### Intended Outcome

Epic 3 was meant to turn the case-study route from a proof entry into a real analytical product: one bounded supply-chain decision view, one supporting cross-functional read, and visible freshness context that made the public slice trustworthy.

### What We Completed

- Story 3.1 framed the business problem, team tensions, and intended reading sequence in business language.
- Story 3.2 established the primary supply-chain decision view with bounded KPIs, policy signals, and a short action queue.
- Story 3.3 added a supporting demand-pressure view showing concentration by account pattern, product family, and current-month weight.
- Story 3.4 added freshness and approved-package context so the case reads as governed release output rather than live operations.

### What Went Well

- The project found a clearer thesis mid-epic and actually used it. Reframing the product as supply-chain decision support immediately made the case easier to reason about.
- The main analytical hierarchy became much stronger: one primary view, one supporting demand view, and freshness as trust context instead of another dashboard.
- Review remained useful. The strongest corrections improved semantics and analytical defensibility rather than only surface presentation.
- The case-study route now reads like a product with a point of view, not just a website with data on it.

### What Was Harder Than Expected

- Scope pressure was real. The project kept trying to turn into sales analytics, logistics decisioning, and planning logic all at once.
- KPI naming and explanation needed more care than expected. A metric can compile and still be misleading if it overclaims what the data actually proves.
- The public policy layer required honesty. Some useful reading rules were derived in the route itself, but that had to be labeled clearly so the page did not pretend those were governed contract fields.
- The conversation about ERP, planning suites, and Power BI created justified anxiety about whether the project was trying to do too much or the wrong thing.

### Key Discoveries

- The biggest analytical value is in supply chain, not in treating sales and logistics as co-equal product centers.
- The product becomes much clearer when it is described as a decision-support layer rather than as a dashboard showcase or mini planning suite.
- Supporting views should explain the primary decision, not compete with it.
- Freshness is part of analytical trust. Without visible approved-package context, the route risks sounding more live and certain than it really is.
- Power BI and Fabric should come back as technical proof, not as the primary storytelling surface.

### Review Patterns

- The best review findings challenged semantics, not syntax. The most important issues were whether a KPI was honestly named, whether a policy signal was truly governed, and whether two compared numbers lived at the same grain.
- The project improved whenever the question changed from "does this look analytical?" to "is this claim actually defensible from the published slice?"
- Tightening language around public-safe boundaries consistently improved both trust and product clarity.

### Technical Debt and Open Risks

- The case-study route is much stronger, but final copy refinement still remains a separate pass.
- The logistics support view is still deferred; it should not be rushed back in without a bounded contract.
- The technical-method route now needs to catch up with the clarity achieved on the case-study route so technical proof remains equally disciplined.
- Trello sync continues to be operationally useful, but intermittent attachment issues still make it a less reliable mirror than the repo artifacts themselves.

## Next Epic Preparation

### Epic 4 Readiness

Epic 4 was ready because Epic 3 finally stabilized the product thesis. The case is now clear enough that the technical-method route can explain source slice, boundaries, masking, grain, and evidence without floating free of the business story.

### What Epic 4 Needed to Preserve

- Keep the business case primary and the technical proof secondary.
- Keep Power BI or Fabric proof bounded and explicitly labeled if it returns.
- Make technical evidence legible without exposing private prep work or turning the route into a raw repo tour.
- Preserve the distinction between approved public package, candidate lane, and private reconstruction work.

### Action Items

- Owner: Product direction
  Keep the supply-chain decision-support thesis explicit across Epic 4 so technical proof reinforces the product instead of redefining it.

- Owner: Technical method
  Explain source domains, masking, grain, and evidence through bounded public artifacts rather than through generic tool talk.

- Owner: Content and UX
  Continue reducing any copy that sounds like platform evangelism or portfolio philosophy instead of real analytical explanation.

- Owner: Delivery discipline
  Keep review focused on analytical truthfulness, boundary honesty, and what the public slice can actually support.

### Carry-Forward Agreements

- One primary analytical view remains the center of the product.
- Supporting views remain subordinate and bounded.
- Approved-package freshness and release context stay visible wherever analytical claims depend on recency.
- Power BI and Fabric re-enter later as proof of platform credibility, not as replacement for the web experience.

## Closing Summary

Epic 3 was the turning point of the project. It resolved the biggest ambiguity in the product by deciding what the tool is actually for and what it is not. The most valuable outcome was not just the new route sections, but the stronger analytical thesis behind them. That gives Epic 4 a much better foundation for technical proof.
